# A17. Discover 10 different types of locks in use.

**Overview:** A lock is a mechanical or electronic device used to restrict access to a physical space or object. They vary widely in how the work, their security, and use cases. The following 10 physical locks represent a range of locks that are purely mechanical to those that are electronically controlled.

1. **Pin Tumbler Lock**
    - The most common type of lock that is found in most residential doors and padlocks. The mechanism contains a series of spring-loaded pin stacks that blocks the cylinder from rotating. The correct key raises each pun to the exact height that is needed to align at the shear line, which allows the cylinder to turn and the lock to open.
    - Vulnerable to lock picking and key duplication
2. **Combination lock**
    - Uses a sequence of numbers instead of a key. A rotating dial turns the internal discs. When all the notches align, the shackle releases. Alternative versions of these locks use buttons and require a specific sequence of buttons to be pressed. This eliminates key duplication
    - Vulnerable to shoulder surfing or brute force if there the attacker has time or if the sequence of numbers is too short.
3. **Deadbolt lock**
    - A door lock (often paired with other locks) where a solid steel bolt is manually extended into the door frame by turning a key or a thumb turn. This approach is more resistant to forced entry than the normal pin tumbler locks.
    - Vulnerable to lock picking
4. **Time Lock**
    - A time lock uses a clockwork or electronic timing mechanism to prevent a lock from opening until a set time, regardless of any keys or combinations. Commonly used in bank vaults, meaning the timer must reach 0 before the vault can be opened, preventing the vault to be immediately accessible.
5. **Disc Detainer Lock**
    - This lock uses rotating discs instead of pins. The correct key will rotate each disk to align all notches, which releases the lock. This is more resistant to lock picking when compared to pin tumbler locks as it requires specialised tools. This is commonly used in high security padlocks and bicycle locks.
6. **Cam Lock**
    - A small lock used in cabinets, lockers and furniture. Turning the key will rotate a flat metal cam that blocks or clears a locking bar. This is a simple mechanism that offers only low to moderate security and only intended as a deterrent against casual access.
7. **Mortise Lock**
    - A lock that is fitted into a pocket that is cut into the door edge, making it harder to attack physically. It often combines a spring latch and deadbolt into one unit. Uses a lever tumbler mechanism where lever must be raised to the correct height before the bolt moves. This is commonly found in commercial buildings and hotels.
8. **Magnetic Lock**
    - Uses an electromagnet mounted on the door frame that attracts a metal plate on the door, creating a strong holding force (used for Trinity fenced gates). It opens only when power is cut (ideally only through an access control system). This makes it fail-safe by default and makes it ideal for emergency doors as they can open automatically when the power is cut. This removes the lock picking vulnerability.
9. **Smart Lock**
    - An electronic lock that authenticates via PIN, Bluetooth, NFC or biometrics instead of using a traditional key. Often encrypted to prevent replay attacks. This however introduces cybersecurity risks that are not found in traditional locks.
    - Vulnerable to sniffing, firmware vulnerabilities, and account compromise.
10. **Biometric Lock**
    - Authenticates using a physical characteristic (what you are). Most commonly a fingerprint. A sensor captures the fingerprint, converts it into a mathematical representation, and compares it to the stored fingerprint (also mathematical representation) on the device. This eliminates key duplication risks, but still vulnerable to high quality replicas. The security also depends on the type of biometric, and how the underlying sensor works (e.g. optical sensors vs ultrasonic sensors).
